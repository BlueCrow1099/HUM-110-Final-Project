Technology and Society in The Hunger Games

What Is The Hunger Games?

The Hunger Games is a book series written by Suzanne Collins and published under Scholastic. The books have been adapted into movies, but for the sake of keeping things simple, only information provided by the original source material will be referenced here. And while we're on the topic of sources, all art and diagrams shown have been drawn by yours truly.

Back to The Hunger Games, the first book which aptly titled the entire series, was released in 2008. The sequel, Catching Fire, released the following year, and the third book, Mockingjay, released a year after that. All three books in this initial trilogy center on main character Katniss Everdeen, while the two prequel books released much later, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes (2020) and Sunrise on the Reaping (2025), focus on the backstories of Coriolanus Snow and Haymitch Abernathy respectively. While each of these books have different plots, all of them focus greatly on one of the most major pieces of world building in the series, which is also its namesake: The Hunger Games.

Every year, in the fictional country of Panem, the Hunger Games take place. As a reminder to the districts of Panem that once tried and failed to rebel against the Capitol, one boy and one girl aged anywhere from twelve to eighteen are chosen at random from each of the twelve districts to participate in a survival-killing game. The twenty-four chosen children, referred to as tributes, are placed within a controlled area where they must survive. There can only be one victor, and the tributes are allowed to kill each other in order to win. The tributes are chosen through a paper-slips-of-names-in-a-bowl system that is somewhat reminiscent of that in the short story "The Lottery" by Shirley Jackson. However, a person can enter their name extra times. For each extra time that the child enters their name, they earn one tessera. One tessera is worth a year's supply of grain and oil for one person. The amount of times that a child's name is in the bowl is cumulative, so even if they never signed up for tesserae, a child who's never been chosen would have six paper slips with their name on it when they're eighteen, assuming that they haven't been chosen before. The day that the tributes' names are pulled from the bowl is referred to as Reaping Day, and takes place on July 4th. Fittingly, having your name pulled to be a tribute is referred to as being reaped. You can, however, also volunteer as tribute. This is usually done by tributes from District 1 and District 2, as they are trained in special academies their whole life for these games. These specially-trained tributes are called Careers, and they usually win the Games. In addition to all of this, every twenty-five years, a Quarter Quell occurs. This means that the Hunger Games for that year will have some extra factor that will make it harder and more exciting to watch, as canonically, the Games are broadcast on live TV and are required viewing for the citizens of Panem. Game victors are traditionally immune from being reaped again.

In addition to forming a timeline with the five currently existing books, it is also implied that Panem was once part of or simply was the United States. Signs are as follows:

  1. In The Hunger Games, it is explicitly stated that Panem is in North America, saying "In the ruins of a place once known as North America lies the nation of Panem, a shining Capitol surrounded by twelve outlying districts" in the book's blurb that you can find on the back of a paperback copy or on the inside of the book jacket in a hardcover.

  2. In Sunrise on the Reaping, it is revealed that Reaping Day is July 4th, saying "Fifty-some years ago, the districts rose up against our Capitol's oppression, kicking off a bloody civil war in Panem. We lost, and in punishment every July 4th, each of the districts routinely has to send two tributes, one girl and one boy between the ages of twelve and eighteen, to fight to the death in an arena" (Collins 17). July 4th is Indepence Day for the US, and seeing that the author herself is from Conneticut, that's likely on purpose.

  3. Also in Sunrise on the Reaping, the poem "The Raven" by Edgar Allen Poe is integral to character development, recited, and the canonical reason that a character has her name (Lenore Dove Baird) and hence canonically exists within the world of Panem. Edgar Allen Poe was an American horror poet. The reciting of the poem starts in Chapter 27 on page 366 and ends on page 380.

With all of these signs in mind, let's assume that at least part of Panem was part of the US. We can then assume that Panem has the same history with technology as the US in real life does. Premise

The second-latest book that ironically takes place the earliest in the timeline, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes shows the origins of Coriolanus Snow as he navigates mentoring District 12 tribute Lucy Gray Baird for the 10th Annual Hunger Games, academy life, and trying to turn his family name back into one that carries power behind it. The book does not show how he became president of Panem; rather, it shows how sixteen-year-old Coriolanus became the ruthless, unforgiving person that he is as President Snow in the original Katniss trilogy.

Why The Hunger Games?

With all of this context and analysis done, I do want to add a short section here to explain why I chose this series of books for this project. I know it's not required, but it's something I want to talk about.

The Hunger Games series has had a serious impact of its own. This series came about during the 2000s and is credited with having a major influence over future YA dystopian books, TV shows, and movies. But not often enough, I think, is Suzanne Collins given her flowers for how much thought she put into this series. Many series, some being very popular, get critisized for adding character appearances, gore, language, or other mature content just for shock factor or the sake of raising the age rating. But what Collins does with this series, in the opinion of myself and many others, is that she doesn't add content or extra books at all without purpose. The Hunger Games books are especially interesting to analyze bnot just because the characters and themes are complex and the world building is thought out, but because the author's purpose is not hard to identify but is heavily layered. In Sunrise on the Reaping, for example, Collins explores implicit submission and propaganda. She opens the book with quotes from George Orwell, William Blake, and David Hume. Then, through the story, Haymitch is constantly asked by Plutarch two things: Why don't the districts rebel, and why can't he imagine waking up to a tomorrow where the Hunger Games don't exist? These two questions are used to tackle the implicit submission, and then over the course of events in the book, we are shown how much of a horrific mess the 50th Annual Hunger Games are, how the Capitol uses heavy editing to cover said mess up, and how propaganda has affected how the Capitol citizens view the Hunger Games in different ways. And as the cherry on top, it all manages to tie back into A Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes and/or Katniss' trilogy.

With so much detail put into how these books are structured so that Collins can get her message aross and how many doors these books opened for future writers, it then becomes all the more important to know what parts of the history of technology and society have influenced this series or even made it possible in the first place. Yes, Collins doesn't sugarcoat her intentions, and yes, she ties in literature that inspired her (in more recent books), but that's only scratching the surface. While it's easy to identify the author's purpose in this series, you're also not spoonfed how every detail correlates with each other or with the real world. Those connections are for the reader to make for themself in order to truly understand the text. The Hunger Games is not just a thrilling series with action and strategy and emotional. It is also a series of commentaries from Collins on human society made by pushing it to an extreme level of corruption and despair while still not forcing the reader to give too much into the suspension of disbelief --- the series is fictional, but not unbelievable. It maintains a certain level of connection with reality by having world building that connects to real life and characters that have many dimensions.

Case and point, a deeper understanding and ability to analyze this series requires knowing historical context, especially that of technology and society, from real life. So what better series to use for a HUM-110 final project than this?

Technology and Society in The Hunger Games

What Is The Hunger Games?

The Hunger Games is a book series written by Suzanne Collins and published under Scholastic. The books have been adapted into movies, but for the sake of keeping things simple, only information provided by the original source material will be referenced here. And while we're on the topic of sources, all art and diagrams shown have been drawn by yours truly.

Back to The Hunger Games, the first book which aptly titled the entire series, was released in 2008. The sequel, Catching Fire, released the following year, and the third book, Mockingjay, released a year after that. All three books in this initial trilogy center on main character Katniss Everdeen, while the two prequel books released much later, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes (2020) and Sunrise on the Reaping (2025), focus on the backstories of Coriolanus Snow and Haymitch Abernathy respectively. While each of these books have different plots, all of them focus greatly on one of the most major pieces of world building in the series, which is also its namesake: The Hunger Games.

Every year, in the fictional country of Panem, the Hunger Games take place. As a reminder to the districts of Panem that once tried and failed to rebel against the Capitol during the Dark Days, one boy and one girl aged anywhere from twelve to eighteen are chosen at random from each of the twelve districts to participate in a survival-killing game. The twenty-four chosen children, referred to as tributes, are placed within a controlled area where they must survive. There can only be one victor, and the tributes are allowed to kill each other in order to win. The tributes are chosen through a paper-slips-of-names-in-a-bowl system that is somewhat reminiscent of that in the short story "The Lottery" by Shirley Jackson. However, a person can enter their name extra times. For each extra time that the child enters their name, they earn one tessera. One tessera is worth a year's supply of grain and oil for one person. The amount of times that a child's name is in the bowl is cumulative, so even if they never signed up for tesserae, a child who's never been chosen would have six paper slips with their name on it when they're eighteen, assuming that they haven't been chosen before. The day that the tributes' names are pulled from the bowl is referred to as Reaping Day, and takes place on July 4th. Fittingly, having your name pulled to be a tribute is referred to as being reaped. You can, however, also volunteer as tribute. This is usually done by tributes from District 1 and District 2, as they are trained in special academies their whole life for these games. These specially-trained tributes are called Careers, and they usually win the Games. In addition to all of this, every twenty-five years, a Quarter Quell occurs. This means that the Hunger Games for that year will have some extra factor that will make it harder and more exciting to watch, as canonically, the Games are broadcast on live TV and are required viewing for the citizens of Panem. Game victors are traditionally immune from being reaped again.

While a year for any of the books to take place in is never explicitly stated, (allegedly Suzanne Collins stated in a 2010 interview with Shelf Awareness that the Katniss trilogy kicks off roughly eighty years into the future, but I could not find the actual interview to back up that claim,) a chronology of the five released books can be formed.

In addition to forming a timeline with the five currently existing books, it is also implied that Panem was once part of or simply was the United States. Signs are as follows:

  1. In The Hunger Games, it is explicitly stated that Panem is in North America, saying "In the ruins of a place once known as North America lies the nation of Panem, a shining Capitol surrounded by twelve outlying districts" in the book's blurb that you can find on the back of a paperback copy or on the inside of the book jacket in a hardcover.

  2. In Sunrise on the Reaping, it is revealed that Reaping Day is July 4th, saying "Fifty-some years ago, the districts rose up against our Capitol's oppression, kicking off a bloody civil war in Panem. We lost, and in punishment every July 4th, each of the districts routinely has to send two tributes, one girl and one boy between the ages of twelve and eighteen, to fight to the death in an arena" (Collins 17). July 4th is Indepence Day for the US, and seeing that the author herself is from Conneticut, that's likely on purpose.

  3. Also in Sunrise on the Reaping, the poem "The Raven" by Edgar Allen Poe is integral to character development, recited, and the canonical reason that a character has her name (Lenore Dove Baird) and hence canonically exists within the world of Panem. Edgar Allen Poe was an American horror poet. The reciting of the poem starts in Chapter 27 on page 366 and ends on page 380.

With all of these signs in mind, let's assume that at least part of Panem was part of the US. We can then assume that Panem has the same history with technology as the US in real life does. With that assumption in mind, let's make some connections between Suzanne Collins' The Hunger Games and some developments in technology and society taught in HUM-110. For each book, one piece of technology important to the plot and/or character arcs will be reviewed, analyzed, and compared with another piece of technology that was a course concept in HUM-110. This overview and analysis will go in the chronological order of the books, so it will start with The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes and end with Mockingjay.

Needless to say, this will contain spoilers for all books in the series as of 2026.

The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes (2020)

Premise

The second-latest book that ironically takes place the earliest in the timeline, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes shows the origins of Coriolanus Snow as he navigates mentoring District 12 tribute Lucy Gray Baird for the 10th Annual Hunger Games, academy life, and trying to turn his family name back into one that carries power behind it. The book does not show how he became president of Panem; rather, it shows how eighteen-year-old Coriolanus became the ruthless, unforgiving person that he is as President Snow in the original Katniss trilogy.

Technology and Society

Story Context

Right when the story opens, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes establishes Coriolanus' home life. Even though he lives in the Capitol and will one day be the corrupt President Snow of Panem, when the book starts, he lives in poverty with his older cousin, Tigris, and their grandmother, whom they refer to as Grandma'am. The Snows were once a more powerful family, but the war with the districts during the Dark Days combined with Coriolanus' father dying in the forests of District 12 and his mother dying after giving birth to his sister (who died not long later) ruined them.

It's showed early on that Coriolanus tries his best to hide the current financial state of his family. When he is panicking that Tigris might not have been able to repair his father's old shirt to a wearable state, the book states, "If Tigris' revamped shirt was unwearable, what was he to do? Fake the flu and call in sick? Spineless. Soldier through in his uniform shirt? Disrespectful. Squeeze into the red button-down that he had outgrown two years ago? Poor. Accptable option? None of the above" (Collins 6).

Even with the Snows still facing money struggle ten years after the war ended, Coriolanus and Tigris still found themselves hopeful that, one day, the Snows would climb their way back up the ranks of Panem. As much is illustrated when Tigris shows Coriolanus how she's repaired his father's shirt to a classy state and the text says,

" 'You're brilliant,' he said earnestly. 'And the best cousin ever.' Careful to hold the shirt out of harm's way, her hugged her with his free arm. 'Snow lands on top!'

" 'Snow lands on top!' Tigris crowed. It was the saying that had gotten them through the war, when it was a constant struggle not to be ground into the earth" (Collins 9).

In the first appearance of the phrase, "Snow lands on top" is used in a harmless way that simply expresses hope for the future of the Snows. But that meaning shifts to be more sinister as the story goes on. Coriolanus recites this phrase a few more times in this book, but each time, you see him become more corrupt as he forces his way into power with selfish and greedy actions. The last time is said is in the epilogue as the very last line of the book, after Coriolanus has betrayed everyone who thought they were his friend, after he has secured his position as Dr. Gaul's (a talented scientist and Gamemaker) apprentice, after he has sworn to never fall in love again, and after he has killed Dean Highbottom with rat poison that the text says,

"Although he couldn't help hoping that, as the dean drew his final breath, he'd realize what so many others had realized when they'd challenged him. What all of Panem would know one day. What was inevitable.

"Snow lands on top" (Collins 517).

Course Connection

The evolution of the use of "Snow lands on top" by Coriolanus, especially at the end of the book, is highly reflective of Social Darwinism, a Chapter 6 course concept. From the late 1800s to early 1900s, as millionaires rose and the distance between the rich and the working class grew in the US, Social Darwinism was used to justify the sheer wealth that a select few in America had gained. In summary, Social Darwinism takes the idea of "Survival of the Fittest" that Darwin used to explain biological evolution and applies it to peoples' financial status. It said that the rich and wealthy had earned their money, and converseley, that the middle and poor class simply didn't work hard enough. In Social Darwinism, only the top survive.

After the point of the excerpt shown above, Coriolanus uses "Snow lands on top" with "top" holding the same meaning as it does in Social Darwinism. To Coriolanus, the top means hightened economic (and eventually political) status. This is the mindset that he learned via his environment in the Capitol: That he must reach the top. It is primary motivation behind his actions throughout the book, the endgame to all of the things, heinous or not, that he does. Coriolanus feeds directly into the "Survival of the Fittest" mindset by killing those who get in his way to the top, whether they meant to (ex. Dean Highbottom) or not (ex. Sejanus). When you look at Coriloanus' actions with Social Darwinism in mind, his repetition of "Snow lands on top" throughout the story becomes more than just wordplay --- it becomes essential to understanding his character arc.

My Review

I first read the entire Hunger Games series about a year ago. I read all of the books back-to-back, in the order that they were released, within the span of two or so weeks. So after reading Katniss' trilogy, I was unsure of what to expect from The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes, as I knew nothing about it other than that it was long. And I, personally, loved the book. What I in particular enjoyed about it is that it's not a villain origin story in the sense of "how did the villain get power?" as much as it is "how did the villain become this mess up in the head?"

Originally, I was simply going to give my own analysis of the book without comparing notes. But a 1-star review left by Emily May on Goodreads has brought up a few points that I'd like to counter, as some of her points as to why she did not like the book are points as to why I did, and other points of hers, I think, are missing the point of the book entirely. Firstly, here is her review:

I feel absolutely drained by this book.

Because I was such a big fan of The Hunger Games trilogy, I was determined to finish The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes, but doing so took a LOT out of me. What a chore this book was! I now understand the disappointed and outraged reviews of others who read it first, but what I don't understand is how those people were still able to blast through this in a day or two. You guys are far tougher readers than I am.

I have to confess that I was initially enthusiastic about this book being told from Coriolanus Snow's perspective. I know it put some people off, but one of the few things I love more than a good villain is a good villain origin story. No, I didn't expect to sympathise with him, but could I understand how he'd gotten so messed up and evil? Well, maybe.

But... I don't think it works. For so many reasons, but a major one for me is that this book is so boring. So meandering, unfocused, dry. There's a lack of urgency and emotion. A lack of any connection with the characters outside of Snow, who is so self-serving and self-pitying that I couldn't even have fun hating him. I could not understand what we were reading for. I had no real questions that needed answering. This book added nothing to the Panem universe.

And the "romance" was just downright unpleasant.

The plot takes us back to the 10th Hunger Games, where Coriolanus Snow is assigned as the mentor to the district 12 candidate, Lucy Gray (whose songs are the one shining light in this novel). Coriolanus sees this as an opportunity to shake himself free of the hardships of the past and improve his social status. Lucy's potential victory becomes deeply-entwined with Coriolanus's own, and their relationship is a discomfiting mix of romantic feelings and him using her to achieve his own means.

But, still, while this is unpleasant, I don't think I am half as bothered by it as I am by how utterly dull the story is. I'm not so refined as to be above some trashy drama, but that's the thing: it's not dramatic. It's lifeless and cold. 90% of the plot exists inside Snow's head. He tells us about the supposed hardships he has had to endure, but we never really feel them. And something about his perspective makes every other character he encounters seem dull also.

A few others noted that this book picks up at the end, which is possibly the only thing that carried me through. It does, but I can also say it was far too little and far too late for me. I think the only good thing about my lack of connection with this book is that I can safely say I don't even consider this part of the same universe as the original trilogy. I can now go forget about it.

The first point of Emily May's that I'd like to discuss is her calling the book draining, "what a chore," and "utterly dull." Obviously, we are all entitled to our own opinions, and TBoSaS is the longest of all the Hunger Games books at 528 pages long with a very tiny font. At some parts of the book I even found myself wonder how much I had left to read, but I was still invested (also, admittedly, I was trying to finish it so that I could move on to SotR for my book club). If lengthy books are not your thing, then yes, you may find TBoSaS a bit of a chore to read. Emily May also refers to specifically the story of the book as "utterly dull" and "not dramatic." This piece of her opinion, I think, stems from something about the book that I personally loved: It's more character-centered than plot-centered. And because it is that way, and Coriolanus lives in the Capitol instead of the districts, yes, the story will be less dramatic than the Katniss trilogy. Coriolanus doesn't play in the life-or-death scenario that is the Games, so there is inherently less shock value, and while I won't delve too deep into this until I discuss SotR, I'll go ahead and say it here: Suzanne Collins doesn't add things into The Hunger Games simply for the sake of adding shock factor. It's something that I've observed and other readers have also observed --- if it doesn't add value to either Collins' message or to her characters, then she doesn't add it just for the sake of it.

The next point of Emily May's review that I'd like to pick apart is the piece where she says that there is a "lack of any connection with the characters outside of Snow, who is so self-serving and self-pitying that I couldn't even have fun hating him." I believe here that Emily May has missed one of the points of the book, and the point is this: Coriolanus Snow is an unreliable narrator. Within the world of writing, an unreliabel narrator is exactly what it sounds like --- the character's POV that we are observing the story through is not telling it in a truthful light due to their own warped perception of reality. In the case of Coriolanus, he is an unreliable narrator because he sees the world, like I said before in my course connection, through a Social Darwinist perspective, and that motivates his actions. And when he feels any real connection with another character, like Lucy Gray, he can't, or won't, find it in himself to be redeemed. And Emily May is right: Coriolanus is self-serving and self-pitying. He gets Sejanus killed for treason and then has a breakdown at his Peacekeeper locker as if it isn't his own fault. When Lucy Gray realizes that Coriolanus is the one that got Sejanus executed and disappears at the climax of the book, Coriolanus decides that Lucy Gray manipulated him and swears to never love again, and that if he is to get married, it will be to someone he hates so that he cannot be "manipulated" again. So we are not supposed to have any connection to these characters simply based on what Coriolanus Snow's connection to them is, because he is self-serving, self-pitying, and unreliable as a narrator. The reader's connection to the other characters is based on the reader understanding this and being able to differentiate how Coriolanus sees the people around him from what the trust of the situation really is.

Next, I want to point out how egregiously Emily May missed the point of Coriolanus' relationship with Lucy Gray. In her review, Emily May calls the romance between the two characters "downright unpleasant," with "romance" being in air quotes. Then, at the end of her review, Emily May says "their relationship is a discomfiting mix of romantic feelings and him using her to achieve his own means." So let's be very clear: Coriolanus does try to use Lucy Gray to achieve his own goals, which is the win the 10th Annual Games and hence secure the Plinth Prize so that his family has the money to be of high social status once again. After he is caught cheating in the Hunger Games, he is punished by being sent to be a Peacekeeper. He then uses the last of him money to make sure he is sent to District 12 when he was not originally supposed to be, and he does this because Lucy Gray did win the Games and was sent back to District 12, and he did have real feelings for her even after she no longer became useful to his goals. At the end of the book, Lucy Gray disappears into the woods even though they were supposed to run away together becuase she's realized that Coriolanus is why Sejanus is dead. In order to disappear, she lays a trap for Coriolanus and uses the jabberjays' ability to mock her singing to through him off her trail. The book ends with the reader not knowing the fate of Lucy Gray because Snow does not know the fate of Lucy Gray. Snow decides that he hates love and Lucy Gray. So, yes, their romance is ultimately doomed and involved Coriolanus seeing Lucy Gray as a means to an end at the beginning. But based on the way she phrased it, Emily May uses this as a point of critism as if to say that the romance should have been more along the lines of a traditional romance. For the sake of who Snow is, however, it cannot be that way, because their relationship establishes two things: One, that Coriolanus Snow had the opprotunity to not be evil, and two, that he did not take it. And the way he looks back on his relationship with Lucy Gray with such resentment highlights exactly how self-centered he is, because somehow, at the end of it all, he views himself as the victim.

All of this cumulates to my last critism of Emily May's review: Where she says that this book "added nothing to the Panem universe," because that statement is wrong. This book adds, arguabley, one of the most important things to the world of The Hunger Games: Insight into the motivations of President Snow, who is the primary antagonist throughout the series. Because when you read this book fully through, and then look again at the Katniss trilogy and Haymitch's book, you realize that not only is Snow very self-centered, power-hungry, and have a victim complex, but he is, at his core, a petty man. Yes, he is a ruthless dictator who wants to maintain his status as head of Panem, but when it comes down to it, a lot of his hatred for specific characters in the series stems from his failed situationship with Lucy Gray. The books go out of their way to show this, for example, by establishing several parallels between Lucy Gray and Katniss Everdeen. Both come from District 12 and, against all odds, won their Games. Both have a little sister-figure in their lives that they love and look after (Katniss has Prim and Lucy Gray has her little cousin, Maude Ivory). When Coriolanus and Lucy Gray are by a lake together, Lucy Gray shows him some swamp potato and tells him that she prefers to call it katniss. And when Lucy Gray uses the jabberjays to repeat her song as she runs away from Coriolanus and disappears at the end of the book, she gets them to sing her song "The Hanging Tree" which irritates Snow because he already knows that she knows that he's why Sejanus is dead. But again, the first time that "The Hanging Tree" is sung in this series, if you read the books in the order of their release, is by Katniss. Peeta even says that the moment he fell for Katniss was in kindergarten because she had the best singing voice, and Lucy Gray's singing does play into her relationship with Coriolanus.

Sunrise on the Reaping (2025)

Premise

The most recent installment of the series, (as of the time of typing this,) Sunrise on the Reaping tells the tale of how Haymitch Abernathy became the seccond District 12 tribute in history to win the Hunger Games. The series of unfortunate events that is Haymitch's story starts with having Reaping Day for a birthday and being forced into the 2nd Quarter Quell, which has the special additional rule that has each district send in four tributes (two boys and two girls) instead of two (one boy and one girl). And, throughout the story, Haymitch is forced to confront two important and not easily answered questions: Why don't the districts rebel, and can he ever dain to imagine a world without the Hunger Games?

Technology and Society

Story Context

In Chapters 26 and 27 of Sunrise on the Reaping, we see the immediate and near-future of Haymitch's life after winning the 50th Annual Hunger Games. When he arrives back in District 12, his house is burned down, his mother and little brother Sid dead. Not long after, when he reuinites with his girlfriend Lenore Dove, she dies. She died because Haymitch unknowingly fed her gumdrop candies that were poisoned by President Snow, and once the two realized it, it was too late, as Lenore Dove had already swallowed one and couldn't throw it up (she hadn't eaten in days so there was nothing to throw up). After this, Haymitch has PTSD from this and the Games. He becomes an alcoholic to the point that he has hallucinations of Lenore Dove that he talks to, and he drives away any friends he had left by throwing rocks at them from the window of his house in Victor's Village out of fear of them dying too if they get too close.

Course Connection

What Is Man is a book by Mark Twain that critisizes the Gilded Age. At one point in the book, Twain talks about the issue of the wealth gap, explaining the gap and why he believes it exists. According to Twain, the wealth gap exists because certain people are trained in what society deems as the "right" direction while others are not. These people that are trained in the "right" direction are the ones that gain wealth and power. The world congratulates and awards them for this, but to Twain, there is not real personal merit in the climb to the top. Society simply acts like there is in order maintain the wealth gap.

Similar to how Twain believes there is no personal merit in how the upper class gain their wealth, The Hunger Games books constantly remind the reader that there is no winner of the Games because after you win, the Capitol makes you suffer. And like how Twain says that society awards the rich despite the lack of personal merit, the Capitol frames winning the Games as some sort of grand honor and path to being treated like you're one of them. Sunrise on the Reaping does this best because after Katniss' victories, we see her join the rebellion in District 13. But for a long time after his Games, Haymitch does not get to have a happy ending. Of course, Katniss' books are still highly important to understanding this theme. In Sunrise on the Reaping, you learn that another victor, Beetee, is being punished after his games via his son being reaped and Beetee being the one to mentor him. Beetee and Haymitch both tried to protest against the Capitol and destroy the Hunger Games while they were in the arena, so the argument can be made that President Snow punished them specifically for that. However, during Katniss' trilogy, Finnick Odair and Johanna Mason are introduced. They are also Hunger Games victors, and you learn that after their Games, they were both trafficked for sex by the Capitol. The books never say whether they did or didn't rebel during their Games, but we do know that Finnick was fourteen when he won his, and that Johanna's family was also killed by the Capitol. Even with that extra knowledge that victors were punished regardless of winning, however, Sunrise on the Reaping delves the most into there being no winner of the Games because, once again, you see it happen to Haymitch through Haymitch's perspective instead of through Katniss when she's being told what happened by others.

The Hunger Games (2008)

Premise

The book that started it all, The Hunger Games follows the story and struggles of sixteen-year-old Katniss Everdeen as a tribute for the 74th Annual Hunger Games after she volunteers as tribute in place of her twelve-year-old sister, Primrose, who she became more of a mother-figure to due to their actual mother becoming less attentive after their father died in a mining accident.

Technology and Society

Story Context

After the tributes for each district are chosen, a few days are spent advertising them to Capitolites who may want to be sponsors to specific tributes they think will win (sponsors send gifts like food, water, and medicine into the arena for a chosen tribute). After bringing the tributes to the capitol, the first event is an opening ceremony where the tributes ride into the City Circle in chariots, where President Snow officially welcomes them to the Capitol from a balcony above the Circle. There are six chariots, one for each district, and the tributes are dressed in matching uniforms that represent their district. Katniss explains this in Chapter 5, when she says via her narration,

"For the opening ceremonies, you're supposed to wear something that suggests your district's principal industry. District 11, agriculture. District 4, fishing. District 3, factories. This means that coming from District 12, Peeta and I will be in some kind of coal miner's getup" (Collins 66).

Course Connection

One of the very first concepts learned in HUM-110 is that geography impacts innovation. This means that the location of a society will impact what inventions come from that society due to needing to adapt to the land around it. Societies from the beginning of the Neolithic Age are great examples of this. The Aztecs, for example, built their city on top of a body of water by putting their buildings on bridges and contructing the city in a circular shape. The Inuits, meanwhile, invented snow goggles, igloos, and kayaks in order to live in their much colder environment, as they were located in northern Canada while Aztec City was where modern-day Mexico City is now.

Similarly, the geography of the districts has determined what industry they work in. Each district only works in one. District 4 is by the water, so it's where the fishing industry is. District 12 has easy access to coal mines. Each district, depending on its location, produces something specific and have the technologies for that. This piece of world building returns in bits and pieces through the series. In Catching Fire, Katniss doesn't know what CPR is while Finnick does because there is less need for it in District 12 than 4. In the same book, when we're introduced to Johanna Mason, we learn that her district, District 7, specializes in lumber. And in Mockingjay, it's revealed that District 13's (which was destroyed in the war and thought to have no one living there) industry was weaponary, which is part of why they were destroyed during the Dark Days but the other districts were not.

Why The Hunger Games?

With all of this context and analysis done, I do want to add a short section here to explain why I chose this series of books for this project. I know it's not required, but it's something I want to talk about.

The Hunger Games series has had a serious impact of its own. This series came about during the 2000s and is credited with having a major influence over future YA dystopian books, TV shows, and movies. But not often enough, I think, is Suzanne Collins given her flowers for how much thought she put into this series. Many series, some being very popular, get critisized for adding character appearances, gore, language, or other mature content just for shock factor or the sake of raising the age rating. But what Collins does with this series, in the opinion of myself and many others, is that she doesn't add content or extra books at all without purpose. The Hunger Games books are especially interesting to analyze not just because the characters and themes are complex and the world building is thought out, but because the author's purpose is not hard to identify but is heavily layered. In Sunrise on the Reaping, for example, Collins explores implicit submission and propaganda. She opens the book with quotes from George Orwell, William Blake, and David Hume. Then, through the story, Haymitch is constantly asked by Plutarch two things: Why don't the districts rebel, and why can't he imagine waking up to a tomorrow where the Hunger Games don't exist? These two questions are used to tackle the implicit submission, and then over the course of events in the book, we are shown how much of a horrific mess the 50th Annual Hunger Games are, how the Capitol uses heavy editing to cover said mess up, and how propaganda has affected how the Capitol citizens view the Hunger Games in different ways. And as the cherry on top, it all manages to tie back into A Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes and/or Katniss' trilogy.

With so much detail put into how these books are structured so that Collins can get her message aross and how many doors these books opened for future writers, it then becomes all the more important to know what parts of the history of technology and society have influenced this series or even made it possible in the first place. Yes, Collins doesn't sugarcoat her intentions, and yes, she ties in literature that inspired her (in more recent books), but that's only scratching the surface. While it's easy to identify the author's purpose in this series, you're also not spoonfed how every detail correlates with each other or with the real world. Those connections are for the reader to make for themself in order to truly understand the text. The Hunger Games is not just a thrilling series with action and strategy and emotional depth. It is also a series of commentaries from Collins on human society made by pushing it to an extreme level of corruption and despair while still not forcing the reader to give too much into the suspension of disbelief --- the series is fictional, but not unbelievable. It maintains a certain level of connection with reality by having world building that connects to real life and characters that have many dimensions.

Case and point, a deeper understanding and ability to analyze this series requires knowing historical context, especially that of technology and society, from real life. So what better series to use for a HUM-110 final project than this?
